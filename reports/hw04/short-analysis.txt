I compared A (No RAG), B (Basic RAG), and C (Context RAG) using the same six questions. The results showed that retrieving passages was not enough; those passages had to contain the information needed to answer the question. For Q1, the documents contained the fourteen-day deadline, but the retrieved passages missed it. C refused to answer instead of making up a deadline, but it still failed to answer a question that the documents could support. For Q2, retrieval found information about risk assessments but missed the definition explaining that a lead-based paint inspection locates lead paint.

Q3 worked best. The retrieved passages included the required disclosure information, and C used them to give an answer with supporting citations. Source labels made it easier to connect the answer to its evidence. However, Q2 showed why a citation alone is not enough. An answer can include a citation even when the cited passage does not support the claim.

For the Q2 context sweep, I compared C with one, three, and five retrieved chunks. More chunks added information about inspection opportunities, combined procedures, and disclosure paperwork, but they still missed the inspection definition. None of these settings produced a complete answer supported by the retrieved text. Using fewer chunks saved tokens, but it did not solve the missing-information problem.

For Q5 and Q6, C filtered out passages with low relevance scores and used the required refusal sentence. A and B also declined to give the unavailable rent in Q5. However, both answered the unrelated sports question in Q6 using information outside the documents, and A made up a match score. Q4 was unclear, so C asked which process and jurisdiction the question referred to.

I kept the chunk size setting at 500 characters and the overlap setting at 50 characters. The overlap repeats text between neighboring chunks. I also kept their order the same. No chunks were removed as duplicates or to stay within the allowed amount of context. This means I could not measure the benefits of those settings separately. C also combined several changes: filtering passages, labelling sources, instructions to support answers with evidence, and a separate model that checked whether questions were clear. I therefore cannot say which change caused the improvement. A answered two questions correctly, B answered three, and C answered four out of six. C did best in this test, but six questions are too few to show how reliably it would answer other questions.
